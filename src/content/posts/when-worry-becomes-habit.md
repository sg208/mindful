Many of us worry because it feels like we're doing something—preparing, preventing, controlling. But chronic worry rarely solves problems; it just keeps our nervous system on high alert. The first step is recognizing that worry has become a habit, not a helpful strategy. If it never leads to action and never resolves, it's just mental spinning.

Schedule a short "worry time" each day. When worries pop up at other times, note them and tell yourself you'll consider them during worry time. Often when the time comes, the urgency has faded. For worries you can act on, make a small plan. For those you can't control, practice letting them be. The goal isn't to never worry—it's to contain worry so it doesn't run your whole day.

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is especially effective for persistent worry and anxiety. A therapist can help you challenge unhelpful thoughts and build new patterns. You might never love uncertainty, but you can learn to tolerate it without being consumed by it.
